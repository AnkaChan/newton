02 Energy and implicit Euler gradients

The physical objective lives in hex_energy.py (the Gauss rule, the lumped masses, the stable Neo-Hookean density and the reference module HexImplicitEulerLoss) and is evaluated for mixed-material batches by MixedHexSolverStep._energy in mixed_physics.py. The damping metric shared by both is in damping.py. During training every network query touches this objective three times: once without gradients on the candidate the network is about to see (E_before in run_training), once differentiated with respect to positions only, to build the gradient input of the network (objective_gradient), and once on the fused output positions with the full autograd graph, so that loss.backward() can reach the network weights through the custom fusion backward in fusion.py. The inertial prediction Y is computed once per physical timestep in MixedHexSolverStep.prepare and reused by every inner query of that timestep.

One implicit Euler step as a minimization

Implicit Euler for a deformable body can be written as a minimization: the end-of-step positions X minimize the sum of the stored elastic energy, an inertia term that pulls toward the free-flight prediction Y, and a viscous term that resists shape change since the start of the step. The network is trained to make that minimization cheaper, so its output is scored with exactly this function:

E(X) = E_elastic(X) + E_inertia(X, Y) + E_damping(X, X_start)

E_elastic = sum over cells c and Gauss points q of  w_q * psi(F_cq(X))
E_inertia = sum over corners v of  m_v * |X_v - Y_v|^2 / (2 dt^2)
E_damping = sum over c, q of  w_q * eta * ||F_cq(X)^T F_cq(X) - F_cq(X_start)^T F_cq(X_start)||_F^2 / (2 dt)

X are the shared corner positions being scored, in meters, shape [B, P, 3] with P = 4961 corners in the saved configuration. X_start is the position at the start of the physical timestep and Y its inertial prediction; both are fixed for the whole timestep. All three terms are in joules and every term is returned separately as a [B] tensor, together with their sum, in a HexLossTerms tuple.

Key implementation points:

  • Corner positions are gathered per cell as [B, C, 8, 3] and the first corner of each cell is subtracted before forming the deformation gradient. The Gauss-point deformation gradients have shape [B, C, 8, 3, 3].
  • Each object carries one material tensor [4] = (lambda, mu, rho, eta) registered with its context; the batch stacks them to [B, 4]. lambda and mu are reshaped to [B, 1, 1] so they broadcast against the [B, C, 8] density, and eta against the [B, C, 8] damping density. Masses are stacked to [B, P].
  • The timestep enters as a tensor built from the positions (positions.new_tensor), so the division happens in the positions dtype and device (float32, CUDA in the campaign).
  • The damping branch runs when any object in the batch has eta > 0. The campaign samples eta in [10, 1000] Pa s, so it always runs; objects with zero damping would contribute zero through the multiplication.
  • The mixed step reuses the constant buffers of the reference module (cell corner indices, shape gradients and quadrature weights) and reimplements the same sums per object so that one batch can mix materials; HexImplicitEulerLoss.forward is the single-material version of the same arithmetic.

Y is fixed for the whole physical timestep

The inertia term needs the position the body would reach with no elastic or viscous forces. This is the ordinary explicit prediction from the start of the step:

Y = X_start + dt * V_start + dt^2 * a
a = gravity + external_force / m           (per corner, m/s^2)

dE_inertia / dX_v = m_v * (X_v - Y_v) / dt^2   (a force, N)

Gravity enters the objective only through Y; there is no separate gravitational potential. make_inertial_prediction validates shapes and finiteness and then evaluates the formula with plain tensor arithmetic; the acceleration must broadcast to [B, P, 3].

MixedHexSolverStep.prepare is the physical timestep boundary. It runs on the CPU, on unbatched float32 [P, 3] tensors and under torch.no_grad(), once per timestep and per object:

  • It snapshots X_start (physical_positions), the velocity and the external force into a payload of detached tensors, computes Y with a = gravity + force / mass, and records the prescribed corner positions fixed_positions = X_start[fixed].
  • It also runs the native rigid pose predictor and fuses a zero increment on top of the rigid pose. That rigid candidate is not used for learning: the trajectory factory replaces it with the inertial prediction (optionally with smooth noise, chapter 04) before any network query. Y itself is never replaced.
  • Every inner query of the timestep receives the same inertial_prediction and the same physical_positions; only candidate changes between queries.

When the inner iterations of a timestep are done, advance commits the final candidate as the new physical state: velocity is the finite difference (X_final - X_start) / dt, prescribed corners get zero velocity, and prepare is called again to produce the next Y. With the campaign's dt = 1 / 300 s and gravity = (0, -9.81, 0) m/s^2, the gravitational part of Y - X_start is 1.09e-4 m per step.

Lumped masses

Each rest cell is a cube of side h with density rho. Its mass rho h^3 is split equally among its eight corners:

m_v = sum over cells c that contain corner v of  rho_c * h^3 / 8
  • quadrature_weights.sum() equals h^3, so cell_mass_eighth is rho h^3 / 8 per cell, shape [C]. It is repeated eight times and scattered with index_add_ over the flattened [8 C] corner index list into a [P] mass vector.
  • An interior grid corner belongs to eight cells and receives the full rho h^3; face, edge and vertex corners of the block receive four, two or one eighths. For rho = 1000 kg/m^3 and h = 0.025 m a cell weighs 15.6 g and each eighth is 1.95 g.
  • Prescribed corners keep their positive mass. register_context rejects any nonpositive mass, and the pins participate in the inertia term like every other corner; their positions are enforced by fusion, not by the energy.

Stable Neo-Hookean density

The elastic law is Newton VBD's stable Neo-Hookean density. The sampled physical Lamé parameters (from Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio, chapter 04) are mapped onto the law's own constants as mu_NH = mu and lambda_NH = lambda + mu, and the density is written so that the rest state F = I has zero energy:

J = det F
psi(F) = mu/2 * (||F||_F^2 - 3) - mu * (J - 1) + (lambda + mu)/2 * (J - 1)^2       [J/m^3]
P(F)   = dpsi/dF = mu * F + ((lambda + mu) * (J - 1) - mu) * cof(F)               [Pa]

evaluated from H = F - I with the exact identities
||F||_F^2 - 3 = 2 tr(H) + ||H||_F^2
J - 1        = tr(H) + s2(H) + s3(H)      s2 = sum of the three principal 2x2 minors, s3 = det(H)
psi          = mu/2 * ||H||_F^2 - mu * (s2 + s3) + (lambda + mu)/2 * (tr(H) + s2 + s3)^2

The two terms mu tr(H) that appear when the identities are substituted cancel algebraically, so the last line is the naive density written without them. Near rest H is small and the naive formula would subtract two nearly equal quantities of size mu in float32; the rewritten form keeps only the small quantities. The result is the same function of F.

The shift by mu is what makes the second-order expansion of psi about F = I equal to linear elasticity with the sampled Lamé constants, psi = mu ||eps||_F^2 + lambda/2 (tr eps)^2 + O(|H|^3) with eps = (H + H^T)/2, so small strains respond with the sampled Young's modulus and Poisson ratio; it is also why lambda + 2 mu is the P-wave modulus used in the loss floor below. The Newton VBD kernel derives the same mapping from the small-strain limit, and test_hex_energy.py's test_rest_zero_stress_and_lame_small_strain_hessian checks that the rest Hessian equals the Lamé tensor. Zero rest stress comes from the linear term -mu (J - 1): its derivative -mu cof(I) cancels mu I from the first term, so P(I) = 0 for any lambda_NH. The constant offsets only make psi(I) = 0.

  • Input F is any tensor [..., 3, 3]; the output has the leading shape. The energy code passes [B, C, 8, 3, 3] and materials of shape [B, 1, 1], which must broadcast exactly to [B, C, 8].
  • s3 = det(H) is formed from the first-row cofactors of H, and s2 from the three principal minors, one of which is the first cofactor. No matrix inverse and no logarithm is used, so the value and its autograd derivative are finite for every finite F, including collapse (J = 0), inversion (J < 0) and F = 0. No Gauss point is rejected for the sign of J; only nonfinite inputs raise.
  • The stress P is never written by hand. Autograd differentiates the polynomial above, which yields the closed form shown, using d(det F)/dF = cof(F).
  • lambda must be nonnegative and mu positive (checked when the reference module registers its buffers), so the volumetric coefficient lambda + mu in front of (J - 1)^2 is positive and the density is bounded below. lambda = 0 is allowed; the mu-dependent volumetric terms remain.
  • Units: mu and lambda in pascals, F dimensionless, so psi is an energy density in J/m^3 and the Gauss weight h^3 / 8 turns it into joules.

Eight Gauss points per cell

A trilinear hexahedron does not have one deformation gradient; F varies inside the cell when the eight corners are not related by an affine map. The energy is therefore integrated with the full 2 x 2 x 2 Gauss rule: two sample locations per material axis, eight points per cell, each carrying one eighth of the rest volume.

reference cube  xi in [-1, 1]^3,   Gauss points xi_q = (+-1, +-1, +-1) / sqrt(3),  q = 1..8
corner signs    s_k in {-1, +1}^3,  k = 1..8   (x, y, z order, z fastest)
N_k(xi)         = prod_i (1 + xi_i s_ki) / 8
dN_k/dX_i(xi_q) = s_ki * prod_{j != i} (1 + xi_qj s_kj) / (4 h)        shape_gradients[q, k, i]   [1/m]
w_q             = h^3 / 8                                               quadrature_weights[q]      [m^3]

F_cq = sum_k (x_ck - x_c0) outer dN_k/dX(xi_q)          F_cq[i, j] = d x_i / d X_j
E_elastic = sum_c sum_q w_q * psi(F_cq)
  • hex_gauss_quadrature builds the rule once in NumPy for the rest edge length h (0.025 m here) and the requested precision; the module registers the results as buffers. The factor 1 / (4 h) is 1/8 from the shape function times 2 / h from mapping the reference cube onto a cube of side h.
  • The deformation gradient is one einsum("bcki,qkj->bcqij") between the gathered corners [B, C, 8, 3] and the gradients [8, 8, 3], giving [B, C, 8, 3, 3] with material axes in the columns.
  • Subtracting the first corner of each cell does not change F, because the shape gradients sum to zero over the eight corners (partition of unity). It removes the large common offset of an object that is far from the world origin before the float32 multiply-add, which is why both the energy and the damping code do it.
  • The eight weights sum to the rest volume h^3. There is no reduced integration and no hourglass stabilization; the same rule also defines the fusion operator of chapter 03.

Damping anchored at the physical start

Viscosity is modelled with Newton VBD's metric damping. At each Gauss point the right Cauchy-Green metric C = F^T F stores the squared lengths of the transformed material axes and their dot products; it is unchanged by any rigid rotation of the cell. Damping penalizes how much that metric has changed since the start of the physical timestep:

C_cq       = F_cq(X)^T F_cq(X)
C_start,cq = F_cq(X_start)^T F_cq(X_start)
delta_C    = C_cq - C_start,cq                                   dimensionless, [B, C, 8, 3, 3]

E_damping = sum_c sum_q  w_q * eta * ||delta_C||_F^2 / (2 dt)      [Pa s * m^3 / s = J]
  • damping_metric_difference recomputes both deformation gradients with the same relative-corner einsum as the energy and returns all nine entries of delta_C. The Frobenius norm therefore counts each off-diagonal component twice, which is the convention of the Newton VBD solid damping this term matches.
  • The anchor is physical_positions, the state at the start of the timestep, not the previous inner iterate. It advances once per timestep in advance, so the damping term measures the total deformation rate of the step, not the progress of the optimizer.
  • The function does not detach either operand. In training X_start arrives as a detached batch tensor, so no gradient flows into it; a differentiable rollout could keep that path.
  • Damping is exactly zero for a rigid motion of the start shape and for a candidate equal to X_start. Because C depends on F quadratically, it is not proportional to ||F - F_start||^2; that is why the network receives the axis change R^T (F - F_start) as a separate input block (chapter 01).

Two different gradients of the same energy

Autograd is used on this objective for two unrelated purposes, and they should not be confused. The first is a gradient with respect to positions: a physical force residual, which is fed to the network as an input and reported as a diagnostic. The second is a gradient with respect to the network weights, which runs through the fused positions and the sparse solve and drives Adam.

Position gradient, dE/dX. objective_gradient evaluates the complete objective at a detached leaf of the candidate that requires gradients (positions.detach().requires_grad_(True); it shares the candidate's storage, no copy is made), with Y and X_start detached, and asks autograd for the gradient of the batch sum. Each object's energy depends only on its own positions, so the gradient of the sum is the per-object gradient. Rows of prescribed corners are zeroed and the result is detached again:

g = dE/dX at the candidate,  g[:, fixed] = 0            [B, P, 3]  in newtons
g_v = sum_q w_q * P(F_q) * dN_v/dX(xi_q)   (elastic)
    + m_v * (X_v - Y_v) / dt^2               (inertia)
    + sum_q w_q * (2 eta / dt) * F_q * delta_C_q * dN_v/dX(xi_q)   (damping)

The network works with cell axes, not corner forces, so g is pulled back through the fusion map. HexFusion.project_gradient runs the same transposed sparse solve as the fusion backward on the free rows of g, once per object against that object's factor (the mixed step concatenates the per-object results along the batch axis), and returns one 3 x 3 matrix per cell:

G_axis = unpack(B^T K_ff^-T g_free)                     [B, C, 3, 3], world coordinates, joules
for every axis increment D:  <G_axis, D> = <g_free, fuse(base, D, fixed) - fuse(base, 0, fixed)>

K_ff is the free-free block of the fusion matrix and B the operator that spreads one target increment per cell onto its Gauss points (chapter 03). Because the axis increments are dimensionless, G_axis carries joules. It is expressed in each cell's frame as R^T G_axis, normalized by its per-object RMS (floored at 1e-12) and clipped to +-10 before it becomes the nine-value gradient block of the node input; the natural log of that RMS is a separate scalar input (chapter 01, assembly). The whole path is detached: the network sees the gradient as data, and no derivative of the loss flows back through it.

The Euclidean norm of g over all free corners is reported as force_residual_norm for the pre-update candidate (MixedHexSolverStep.forward) and averaged into mean_force_residual_n in the training report.

Weight gradient, dloss/dtheta. Training calls loss.backward() on the per-update loss of the fused positions. The chain is:

loss -> E(X_fused) -> X_fused -> _FusionSolve.backward -> world increment R (A_target - A) -> network -> theta

forward solve   X_free  = base_free + K_ff^-1 (B t - K_fc (x_fixed - base_fixed)),   X_fixed = x_fixed
backward        grad_t          = B^T K_ff^-T g_free
                grad_base[free] = g_free
                grad_base[fixed]= K_fc^T K_ff^-T g_free
                grad_fixed      = g_fixed - K_fc^T K_ff^-T g_free
  • PyTorch differentiates the energy's tensor operations on the GPU. Each material context owns its own fusion matrix and cached factor, so the fused batch is a torch.cat of one fuse call per object, and autograd hands each object's own _FusionSolve.backward a cotangent of shape [1, P, 3]; its free rows are copied to the CPU as NumPy, packed as an [F, 3] right-hand side and solved with that object's cached PARDISO factor in transposed mode (iparm[11] = 2). One training batch therefore performs B transposed solves, one per object against that object's factor (the function itself is batched: given a [B, P, 3] cotangent it would pack [F, 3 B] columns and solve once). K is symmetric, so the transposed solve equals the forward solve mathematically; the code still requests the exact adjoint.
  • _adjoint_targets returns both the adjoint columns K_ff^-T g_free, reused for the boundary coupling K_fc^T, and the target gradients reshaped to [B, C, 3, 3] with axes in columns, the same layout as the forward input world_axis_increments.
  • The matrix, its factorization and the operators B, K_fc are constants: they depend only on rest topology and material stiffness weights, and nothing differentiates them. The function is marked once_differentiable, so no second-order derivatives exist.
  • The frames R are detached, so grad_t reaches the network through R^T grad_t with R treated as a constant. The candidate positions and prescribed positions are detached batch tensors that do not require gradients, so autograd discards grad_base and grad_fixed; only grad_t continues to the network output and its weights.
  • The trainer checks that every parameter gradient is finite before the Adam step; a nonfinite gradient aborts the run on all ranks rather than being skipped.

Same function, two roles. The network is given dE/dX at the candidate it starts from, projected onto cell axes and normalized, as an input. The loss is E at the fused positions the network produced. Only the second evaluation is part of the graph that loss.backward() traverses; the first is detached data, exactly like the current axes or the inertial offsets. Nothing in the loss asks the network to reproduce the gradient, and there is no residual-squared term.

The LeCO per-update loss

One query is scored on its own post-update energy, divided by the larger of |E_before| (the energy of the candidate it started from) and the material-aware floor, plus a penalty proportional to any normalized increase over that starting energy. The normalization follows the convention of LeCO (LearnedClothOptimizer, abbreviated LCO in notes/References/lco-one-pager.md), the reference learned optimizer for implicit cloth simulation whose training conventions this trainer reuses: the per-update loss below, an asinh of the normalized post-update energy plus a penalty for going uphill, and the RMS normalization of the gradient inputs in chapter 05.

scale = max(|E_before|, floor)                                     detached, [B]
loss  = asinh(E_after / scale) + w * relu((E_after - E_before) / scale),   w = 1

floor = c * 2^-23 * V * (lambda + 2 mu + eta / dt + rho h^2 / dt^2),   c = 1
V     = total rest volume = C h^3 = 4000 * 0.025^3 = 0.0625 m^3
  • E_before is the total energy of batch["candidate"], evaluated under torch.no_grad() immediately before the forward pass, with the same Y and X_start. It is the energy of the exact tensor the network is about to see, not the energy of the first candidate of the timestep. E_after is result.loss.total on the fused positions and is the only input that carries gradients.
  • asinh(x) is linear for small |x| and behaves like sign(x) log(2|x|) for large |x|, so a proposal that has already reached the floor is scored on a linear scale while large energies are scored logarithmically. It is defined for either sign; the scale uses |E_before| for the same reason.
  • The floor is material aware. Every term in the bracket is a modulus in pascals: lambda + 2 mu the elastic P-wave modulus, eta / dt the viscous modulus over one step and rho h^2 / dt^2 the inertial one. Multiplying by V gives the energy of a unit strain of the whole object, and 2^-23 is the float32 unit roundoff, so the floor is roughly the smallest energy difference float32 can resolve for that object. It is computed in float64 and cast to float32; for lambda = 1e6 Pa, mu = 3e5 Pa, rho = 1000 kg/m^3, eta = 100 Pa s it is about 0.013 J, and for the softest sampled materials (lambda = 1e3, mu = 400, rho = 100, eta = 10) about 8e-5 J. c is the configurable energy_floor_scale, 1 in the campaign.
  • The batch of B = 16 per-object losses is averaged and one Adam step is taken per query batch. Afterwards result.positions[i].detach() becomes the next candidate of that trajectory, so each backward pass covers exactly one proposal; queries at later inner iterations or later timesteps are new training examples, not an unrolled graph.
  • Validation computes the same expression in Python for the first update of each sample (mean_normalized_loss), with the same increase weight, so the curves are comparable.

The position gradient as a validation residual

Validation uses the negative free-corner position gradient as the physical force residual. It is the same autograd.grad pattern as the network input, run inside the no_grad validation loop through torch.enable_grad(), on a detached clone of the candidate (.detach().clone(), the one place this path copies the tensor):

r = || g_free ||_2   with  g = dE/dX at the candidate and g[:, fixed] = 0        per sample, newtons
  • The norm is accumulated in float64 over all corners and components of one sample. Rows of prescribed corners are zeroed first, so pin reactions do not count; they need not vanish at equilibrium.
  • It is recorded for every sample at iteration 0 (the initial candidate) and after each of the 100 inner iterations of the frozen-problem phase, and again after every physical step of the rollout phase. Network weights and their existing .grad buffers are untouched by these calls.
  • The checkpoint-selection metric is the mean over the 512 validation samples of the final-iteration residual (_selection), eligible only when no sample failed and every physical trajectory survived. A zero residual is a stationarity condition of E, not proof of a minimum or of long-horizon stability, so energy, displacement and inversion diagnostics are reported next to it.
